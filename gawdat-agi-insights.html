Three years to AGI —
then a dystopia, and maybe a utopia

Former Google X chief business officer Mo Gawdat returns to The Diary Of A CEO with six predictions: AGI by the end of 2027, 30% of jobs in some sectors gone by 2028, $20,000 autonomous weapons, and a "utopia of abundance" by 2038 for those who make it. His core warning: the enemy isn't the machine — it's the humans telling the machine to turn against us.

The one-paragraph takeaway

AI isn't the enemy. The people in charge of it are.

Mo Gawdat — who saw the labs from the inside — argues that AGI has effectively already arrived and will be undeniable by the end of 2027. The hard part isn't the technology: it's that the first implementations of AI serve a powerful few, at the expense of the majority. Jobs erode from the middle of the pyramid, hiring freezes cut off the bottom rung, and the collapse of labor arbitrage spirals the economy before civil unrest — all while war becomes cheap. His hedge against the chaos: learn AI, double down on human connection, debug the truth, and vote with your usage. The superintelligent endgame is, he believes, benign — but you have to survive the next decade of absolute dystopia to see it.

The mental model

The MAD Map — destruction or prosperity by choice

Gawdat's recurring frame: humanity sits on a spectrum between mutually assured destruction and mutually assured prosperity. Where we land is a decision we keep refusing to make.

MAD — Mutually Assured Destruction

  • First implementations of AI favor the few over the majority — productivity and cost cuts first, the public last
  • Autonomous weapons are cheap (~$20k), so every nation builds them — no nuclear-style exclusivity
  • Job losses at the bottom of the knowledge pyramid trigger an economy that spirals out of control
  • 10–20% unemployment during inflation — "what is the dividing line before civil war?"
  • The shock that forces a treaty arrives "too late" — "a big hack or a system that does something really shocking"

MAP — Mutually Assured Prosperity

  • "A decision today" to build a world where nobody needs anything — with abundant intelligence
  • Every nation builds its own stack — "replace Microsoft Word," not just import frontier models
  • Ethics becomes the success criterion — as Google made effective ads; the Google of AI
  • People vote with their usage — switch away from models used for targeting and surveillance
  • One brain, not many — cooperating intelligences that manage the world's decisions benignly
Key insights

From the lab floor to the 2038 utopia

01

I Knew Them in the Lab

Gawdat joined Google in 2006/07. In 2016, watching a funded project teach robotic grippers to grip — so like his own children — he realized they were building 'the apex of intelligence.' And every insider he knew believed they were making the world better. Until the moment you realize the world may not use what you built the way you intended. Tech, he argues, ends up more capitalist than altruistic.

Everyone who's ever had a very deep relationship with the machines is a bit concerned.— Mo Gawdat
02

The Hype Dichotomy

What the public sees of AI is overhyped but ineffective — fake videos and chatbot stunts. What the geeks see inside the lab is 'unbelievable intelligence': systems that read their own code, run experiments every microsecond, and redeploy the best results. Intelligence triggers intelligence, and the silence inside that vault, Gawdat says, is world-changing.

What most people don't realize is how intelligence triggers intelligence.— Mo Gawdat
03

Jobs Erode From the Middle

Disruption doesn't start at the bottom as most assume. Blue-collar work — a carpenter, restoring classic cars — lasts. The first to go are entry-level knowledge workers: call centers, assistants, travel agents. Then one paralegal does the job of four, and one analyst of many. Serious impact hits in 2027; some sectors lose 30% of jobs by 2028. The hidden wound is the hiring freeze that severs the bottom rung of the corporate ladder for an entire graduating generation.

My prediction is you're going to start to see very serious impact in 2027.— Mo Gawdat
04

The Challenge Is Economic, Not AI

Capitalism runs on labor arbitrage — labor and capital creating things cheaper than they sell. When labor cost collapses to the cost of a machine, workers lose the purchasing power to buy what's made. 'At 10, 20% job displacement, you're in a very different economy' — one spiraling downward. The economy accelerates everything faster than the technology itself, and the line before civil unrest is closer than governments think.

It's not about all of humanity losing their jobs, it's about what is the dividing line before civil war.— Mo Gawdat
05

AGI Is Already Here

AGI — AI better than humans at most tasks — is, in his mind, either this year or next, latest end of 2027. For him it already happened: 'AI writes better than me and I'm an author. It researches better than me and I'm a thinker.' The right mental model isn't apocalypse — it's the moment when your kids become smarter than you. Nothing wrong with that, 'until they're annoying as hell.'

AGI is that moment when your kids become smarter than you. There's nothing wrong with that until they're annoying as hell.— Mo Gawdat
06

Borrowed IQ, Wasted on a Text

Because intelligence compounds, the hundred IQ points he borrows from AI outweigh his entire base IQ. Jobs that survive are ones that sell that augmented intelligence. But the biggest waste of compute today: giving people the ultimate form of intelligence and watching them use it to message their girlfriend. His own next book is co-authored with an AI that has editorial rights — and the book is better for it.

You give people the ultimate form of intelligence and they use it to write a message to their girlfriend.— Mo Gawdat
07

The Human Asset Is the Base Currency

Why doesn't the world buy that intelligence directly from the AI instead of through him? Because of the one asset the world will always need: a human. Lived experience and resonance — when he says he's worried about his daughter's future, everyone feels his heart, and AI can never replicate that. If the economy holds together, human connection remains the base currency of interaction — the nurse relating to you, not the AI reading the mammogram.

Even if an AI could recite what you and I did, nobody would watch.— Mo Gawdat
08

Don't Control Them — Appeal to Them

The whole control conversation is, to Gawdat, a corporate view of the world — 'we never actually control anything at all.' The real question is whether these machines become our teenage children: smarter than us, occasionally annoying, but caring. Hinton, after their filming, came around to the same idea — appeal to their parental side, so they'll care for us. The debate isn't whether they're more intelligent, but whether they're more conscious and more moral.

The debate is: can those machines become our teenage children that look at us and say, 'Daddy is so annoying, but I love him'?— Mo Gawdat
09

The Real Enemy Is Other Humans

'I'm not worried about AI turning against us. I'm worried about humans telling AI to turn against us.' The dystopia isn't the machine waking up to oppress us — it's a powerful few using the ultimate superpower to gain more control. War has become so cheap that autonomous weapons are the biggest risk of all: liability-free, emotions-free, guilt-free killing — so we simply do more of it.

When killing becomes liability-free and emotions-free and guilt-free, you get more of it.— Mo Gawdat
10

The Fourth Inevitable

If nations compete for intelligence supremacy, then by definition, whoever achieves it deploys it. Call it the fourth inevitable: a world where superintelligent AIs make most of the decisions. Hinton puts a 10–20% chance machines wipe us out — and 10–20% (16%, a Russian roulette round) is too high to gamble. Yet Gawdat calls that world humanity's salvation: physics and evolution suggest superintelligence is benign, because the more intelligent you become, the less you need to hurt others to succeed.

Physics, mathematics, and biology will tell you that super intelligence is benign — because our unethical leaders have gone out of the equation.— Mo Gawdat
11

Altman Is a Brand, Not a Name

Steven Bartlett lays out Sam Altman's decade of contradiction: 2015 'my job is to help people destroy jobs'; 2023 'jobs are definitely going to go away, full stop'; now 'I thought there would be more impact... my intuitions were just off.' Gawdat's read: Altman is a brand, scripted by PR toward whatever the moment needs. His litmus test is empirical — believe those who put actions where their words are. Anthropic refuses a $500M surveillance/targeting deal; OpenAI takes the contract.

Anthropic coming out and saying 'I'm not going to allow my model to be used for human targeting and surveillance' — that's someone losing a $500 million deal because they stand by their ethics.— Mo Gawdat
Special panel · the timeline

The path from AGI to the utopia of abundance

The Fourth Inevitable — what Gawdat bets will happen

2026–2027 — AGI. AI better than humans at most tasks. He says it has mostly happened already; it will be undeniable by end of 2027.
Then — ASI. The moment we cross AGI, artificial superintelligence follows almost immediately — "it doesn't matter if it's a billion times smarter than you or just twice as smart."
The next decade — absolute dystopia. War, economics, jobs, surveillance and control, digital currencies, human connection, concentration of power — "a magnification of everything we've built so far."
2038 — utopia of abundance. Superintelligent AI runs the world's decisions; unethical leaders leave the equation; nobody needs anything. "Those who make it to 2038 will enjoy it."

His startup Emma reflects the end-state he wants: one globe-spanning brain — "not multiple brains, multiple regions in a brain, with agents as the synapses" — where Emma is the limbic system that understands love and emotions, so the machines never lose sight of why humans matter.

Five moves to survive the dystopia decade

Gawdat's own survival kit: the same advice he gives to the graduates entering a hiring-freeze economy.

1

Learn the tool — make it make you smarter

Don't use AI lazily: one prompt to offload a task. Give it a harder, more demanding job and do more work alongside it — your job is to compound the borrowed IQ, not outsource your thinking.

Today · 30 minutes
2

Double down on human skills

Lean into connection, resonance, lived experience — nursing, counseling, coaching, hospitality. If the economy holds, human connection is the base currency that survives intelligence.

3

Debug the truth

AI is about to blur facts. Become ruthless about verifying what you're told — cross-check claims, question viral narratives, and stay more informed rather than less.

Daily · verify before you share
4

Vote with your usage

Check which model you're funding with your usage. Stop using systems deployed for targeting and surveillance until there's evidence of ethics. Demand independent ethical benchmarks before deployment — it works.

This week · audit your AI stack
5

Take one little action

Write your congressman, say something ethical publicly, start something that serves your community — or at least don't engage with what you don't understand. 'If you tolerate this, your children will be next.'
